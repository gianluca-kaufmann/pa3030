\begin{abstract}
\noindent
The Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework commits nearly 200 countries to
protect 30\% of land and ocean by 2030, up from 17\% terrestrial coverage today. Where new protected
areas will be designated is both an open empirical question and a consequential
policy problem. For conservation planners and policymakers, the answer determines whether the
30$\times$30 target genuinely protects biodiversity or merely expands coverage on
low-conflict land. For financial supervisors and investors, new designations impose
land-use restrictions that strand agricultural, mining, and infrastructure assets,
a source of transition risk that is currently unquantified. This thesis develops a
machine-learning framework that predicts, for any 1~km$^2$ cell, the probability of
formal WDPA designation within a five-year window. LightGBM and Random Forest models
are trained on annual designation records from 2001 to 2019 across South America,
the United States, and Southeast Asia, with forward projections anchored to a 2024
data snapshot, so the five-year forward window spans 2025--2030, directly aligned with the 30$\times$30 policy deadline.
In South America, the Random Forest model achieves an out-of-sample
ROC-AUC of 0.910 and a PR-AUC of 0.548; the top 1\% of predicted pixels captures
69.1\% of all designated pixels, achieving a pixel-year lift of 67.6x over random. Random Forest outperforms
LightGBM on precision-recall metrics across all three regions; LightGBM results
confirm the directional findings. Feature importance analysis suggests that governments tend to designate areas
where protection is economically feasible, ecologically valuable,
and supported by favourable institutional conditions: road-accessible, densely populated, and
high-value agricultural land is systematically excluded from high-risk zones,
while remote areas adjoining existing protected areas attract designation
irrespective of ecological significance. Forward projections show that all three regions fall far
short of 30$\times$30 under business-as-usual designation rates. Cross-continental
transfer is mixed, consistent with region-specific implementation shaped by local
political economies rather than universal ecological gradients. These
results expose a structural gap between where protection is headed and where
biodiversity and climate-stabilisation priorities are concentrated. The calibrated
probability maps provide a spatial input for nature-related transition-risk analysis, 
identifying where future protection is most likely
to constrain land use under current designation patterns.

\medskip
\noindent\keywords{protected areas; 30$\times$30; transition risk; machine learning;
LightGBM; Random Forest; spatial prediction; land-use change; biodiversity policy}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The global expansion of protected areas (PAs) has become a central pillar of
contemporary biodiversity policy. Under the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity
Framework, adopted at COP15 in December 2022, nearly 200 CBD Parties committed to
protecting 30\% of the world's land and oceans by 2030 \citep{cbd2022}. Yet current
coverage remains far below this target. The Protected Planet Report 2024, the latest
biennial global assessment, estimates that protected and conserved terrestrial and
inland-water areas covered 17.6\% of the global total in 2024
\citep{unepwcmc2024}. Meeting the 30$\times$30 target therefore requires rapid and
substantial expansion over the remainder of the decade.

This expansion is not only an ecological and policy challenge; it also has material
economic implications. PA designation often introduces land-use restrictions that can
constrain agriculture, resource extraction, and infrastructure development
\citep{dudley2008}. New designations may therefore create \emph{transition risk} for
businesses, investors, landowners, and policymakers exposed to affected areas
\citep{govind2025}. Related forms of biodiversity regulation can also affect land
markets and development incentives \citep{frank2025}.

Nature-related financial disclosure frameworks increasingly recognise such land-use
transitions as a source of systemic financial exposure, framing policy-driven
land-use change as a relevant transition-risk channel for businesses and investors
\citep{tnfd2023, govind2025}. Acting on this risk requires more than knowing that PA
expansion is likely. It requires knowing \emph{where} future designations are most
likely to occur. Widely used datasets describe current protection status and PA
attributes well \citep{wdpa, bingham2019}, but they are not designed to provide
forward-looking designation probabilities in a form suitable for
financial or policy use. This gap matters for two audiences. For investors and
financial supervisors, missing spatial probability estimates may make transition
risks from 30$\times$30 difficult to quantify and screen.
FSB and NGFS reviews document data and modelling gaps in nature-related financial
risk assessment, including limited granular or geospatial data, underdeveloped
forward-looking scenarios, and early-stage nature-related disclosure
\citep{fsb2024, ngfs2023}.
For policymakers and conservation planners, global syntheses find that
business-as-usual expansion is unlikely to meet 2030 conservation targets, so understanding
where future designations are probable, and how to shift trajectories, is a live planning
issue \citep{palomo2025}.
Both challenges reduce to the same empirical question: \emph{which locations will be
designated as protected areas in the near future, and with what probability?}

Answering this question is feasible because PA designation is not a random process.
Governments have historically protected land in non-random ways, often favouring
remote, low-opportunity-cost areas while only imperfectly aligning new protection
with ecological priority. Protected areas are disproportionately located at higher
elevations, on steeper slopes, in less densely populated areas, and farther from roads
and settlements \citep{joppa2009, venter2018, baldi2017}. This bias reflects the
political economy of conservation: designation is easiest where opportunity costs are
lowest. If past patterns predict future patterns, then geographic covariates can be
used to build a forward-looking transition-risk model. That perspective aligns with
work on protected-area planning under uncertainty \citep{schuster2023}, yet existing
studies remain predominantly retrospective or geographically narrow, leaving a gap
for forward-looking, calibrated probability outputs that can be used as
transition-risk inputs.

This thesis addresses both gaps by developing a multi-region machine-learning
framework that predicts future PA establishment. The framework treats PA designation
as a rare binary transition in a pixel--year panel, trains LightGBM and Random Forest
models on historical designation patterns, calibrates the raw scores to interpretable
probabilities, and deploys the result to generate forward-looking transition-risk maps
for 2025--2030. Three regions are studied: South America (primary model), the 
United States, and Southeast Asia. The analysis produces calibrated
spatial risk scores, forward probability maps under business-as-usual and 30$\times$30
scenarios, estimates of agricultural land area at risk of designation, assessments of
ecological priority overlap, and a feature importance decomposition of the drivers of
designation decisions.

The main results are as follows. In South America, the Random Forest model achieves
an out-of-sample ROC-AUC of 0.910 and a PR-AUC of 0.548. Its predictions are highly
concentrated: the 1\% of pixels assigned the highest risk contains 69.1\% of all pixels that
were newly designated as protected areas in the test period. At this top-1\% threshold, the
model identifies future designations 67.6 times more often than random selection.
Feature importance suggests that predicted designation risk reflects economic
accessibility, ecological priority, and governance capacity: road-accessible, densely
populated, and high-value agricultural land is systematically suppressed from the
high-risk tier.
Forward projections under business-as-usual rates indicate South America would reach
the 30\% target only after approximately 68 additional years from 2024, with the
United States and Southeast Asia falling even further short.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Biodiversity and Conservation Policy}
\label{sec:lit_biodiversity}

Global biodiversity is declining at a rate unprecedented in human history
\citep{diaz2019}. The IPBES Global Assessment reports that human activities have already
substantially altered three-quarters of the Earth's land surface and that as many as
one million plant and animal species face extinction due to interlinked
drivers including land-use change, overexploitation, climate change, invasive species, and
pollution \citep{ipbes2019}.
The loss is not only ecological: biodiversity is essential for food security,
freshwater provision, disease regulation, and climate stabilisation, among many other
ecosystem services \citep{diaz2018}.
The Millennium Ecosystem Assessment distinguishes four broad categories of these
benefits: provisioning, regulating, cultural, and supporting services
\citep{mea2005}.
The economic magnitude of intact natural systems is also large: global valuation
studies place the annual value of ecosystem services in the tens to low hundreds of
trillions of dollars, depending on assumptions about biome values and land-use change
\citep{costanza1997, costanza2014, kubiszewski2017}.
The Dasgupta Review \citep{dasgupta2021} frames biodiversity loss as a macroeconomic
problem, arguing that the global economy is embedded in a natural capital stock
being drawn down faster than it can regenerate, with consequences for long-run human
prosperity.

Area-based conservation, primarily through protected areas and related designations,
is an important policy instrument used to respond to biodiversity loss
\citep{maxwell2020}. Global syntheses show that, where adequately managed, protected
areas reduce habitat loss and maintain higher local biodiversity than comparable
unprotected landscapes \citep{watson2014, gray2016}. However, the
same reviews identify a persistent gap between the formal extent of protection and its
functional effectiveness: many PAs are underfunded, cover ecologically marginal land,
or lack the management capacity to deter illegal activities. The world's most
threatened species and ecosystems frequently remain outside the protected network
\citep{williams2022, chaplin2023}.

These shortcomings provided the motivation for the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity
Framework, adopted at COP15 in December 2022, which commits nearly 200 countries to
protecting 30\% of terrestrial and marine areas by 2030 \citep{cbd2022}. The
latest biennial Protected Planet Report places protected and conserved terrestrial and
inland-water coverage at 17.6\% in 2024 \citep{unepwcmc2024},
meaning governments must designate approximately the same land area as the entire
existing global PA network within five years. The pace and spatial pattern of this
expansion will largely determine whether the 30$\times$30 commitment delivers
meaningful conservation outcomes or, as past trends warn, concentrates new
designations in ecologically marginal areas \citep{joppa2009, venter2018}.

These conservation pressures do not unfold in isolation from the climate agenda.
Climate change is itself an accelerating driver of biodiversity loss: rising temperatures,
shifting precipitation regimes, and more frequent extreme events alter species distributions,
disrupt the seasonal match between interdependent species, and push populations towards 
their physiological limits \citep{thomas2004, urban2015, ipbesipcc2021}.
Under high-emission scenarios, climate change is projected to overtake land-use change
as the primary global driver of biodiversity loss by mid-century \citep{ipbesipcc2021},
reinforcing the urgency of protecting areas that regulate the climate.
The biodiversity and climate crises are therefore intertwined: carbon-dense
tropical forests and peatlands are often also species-rich, so protecting them
serves dual objectives: conserving ecological communities and preserving terrestrial
carbon stocks \citep{ipcc2019srccl}. The Global Safety Net \citep{dinerstein2020}
maps ``climate stabilisation areas'' as a core conservation priority. Carbon revenues could create additional
incentives to protect high-carbon forests, and thus materially narrow the
chronic financing gap facing protected areas \citep{busch2022}. More broadly, climate
and biodiversity governance are becoming increasingly intertwined, with emerging legal
and disclosure frameworks treating them as overlapping sources of transition risk
\citep{boran2024, govind2025}.

\subsection{Biases in Protected-Area Siting}
\label{sec:lit_siting}

A foundational empirical regularity found in conservation literature is that governments
do not designate PAs at random, nor do they reliably prioritise the land most in need
of protection \citep{joppa2009, venter2018, baldi2017}.
Joppa and Pfaff (\citeyear{joppa2009}) provide a global
analysis: across a large sample of countries, PAs are systematically concentrated at
high elevations, on steep slopes, in remote locations far from roads and settlements,
and on land with low agricultural productivity. These characteristics are precisely
those that minimise the opportunity cost of designation, suggesting that governments
protect land where protection is politically and economically easy rather than where
it is ecologically most urgent \citep{venter2018}.

Venter et al.\ (\citeyear{venter2018}) extend this analysis across multiple decades and
show that the bias has been remarkably persistent, with PA systems continuing to expand
preferentially into economically marginal land even as global conservation commitments
have strengthened. Baldi et al.\ (\citeyear{baldi2017}) characterise this pattern as
``opportunity-driven'' siting: designation follows the path of least resistance. Sharma
et al.\ (\citeyear{sharma2025}) further demonstrate that privately managed protected
areas exhibit even greater bias toward low-productivity land than publicly managed PAs, indicating
that the opportunity-cost logic operates across governance types.

The consequence of this pattern is a gap between where protection
is concentrated and where conservation value is highest. Williams et al.\
(\citeyear{williams2022}) find that the current global PA network is insufficient to
safeguard half the world's mammal species from extinction because their ranges fall
disproportionately outside the protected network. Chaplin-Kramer et al.\
(\citeyear{chaplin2023}) show that only 15\% of the ``critical natural assets'', defined as 
ecosystems providing 90\% of nature's contributions to people globally, from freshwater
to carbon storage, currently lie within formal protected areas.

\citet{mouillot2024} similarly show, using a machine-learning framework, that protected
areas occupy a distinct socio-environmental niche: they are disproportionately located in
high-elevation, low-fertility, and island settings, while many biodiversity-rich areas remain
less favourable to conventional PA establishment. This supports the premise of this thesis:
historical PA siting follows systematic patterns in covariate space that can be learned and
used to forecast future designations.

\subsection{Machine Learning for Conservation Policy}
\label{sec:lit_ml}

Machine learning has become a standard toolkit in quantitative conservation science 
\citep{pichler2023}. Most applications target biophysical outcomes: habitat suitability
mapping from species-occurrence data, land-cover change detection from remote-sensing 
imagery, and deforestation-risk modelling from geospatial predictors \citep{ball2022, pichler2023}.
Mayfield et al.\ (\citeyear{mayfield2017}) provide a demonstration that Random Forest
classifiers \citep{breiman2001} trained on freely available satellite and topographic
predictors match the accuracy of purpose-built deforestation models in Mexico and
Madagascar. Their finding that publicly available data are sufficient for high-quality
land-change prediction is directly relevant here, as the predictor set of this thesis relies entirely on such sources.

A methodologically distinct strand applies machine learning to policy-driven land-use
decisions, asking not which habitats are ecologically suitable but which boundaries
or designations governments actually impose.
Greenhill et al.\ (\citeyear{greenhill2024}) train a classifier on geospatial covariates
to predict which rivers and wetlands fall under federal regulatory jurisdiction under
the US Clean Water Act; their model achieves high accuracy and demonstrates that
machine learning can reverse-engineer the implicit spatial decision rules of regulatory
agencies from observed outcomes alone. The task is methodologically analogous to
predicting where governments will establish protected areas.
Kleinberg et al.\ (\citeyear{kleinberg2018}) provide the theoretical underpinning:
in many institutional prediction problems, models trained on historical decisions
substantially outperform unaided human judgment by exploiting high-dimensional
covariate patterns that are too complex for human experts to track consistently.

These studies jointly establish three conditions that motivate the approach of this
thesis. First, government conservation decisions follow systematic spatial patterns
in geographic covariate space. Second, those patterns are recoverable from
historical designation records using standard geospatial predictors. Third, 
gradient-boosted tree models and Random Forests \citep{ke2017, breiman2001} 
are well suited for this task as they handle
non-linear interactions among covariates without requiring prior specification of
functional forms, accommodate rare-event class imbalance through cost-sensitive
training and post-hoc Platt calibration \citep{niculescumizil2005}, and support
post-hoc interpretation via SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values
\citep{lundberg2017}.

Despite this progress, existing machine-learning applications in conservation do not
target the specific problem of predicting near-term PA designation.
Most models are retrospective or descriptive, classifying existing land cover or
regulatory status, or focus on ecological rather than institutional outcomes. Existing
studies do not yet combine a temporal panel structure, calibrated probabilities, and
forward deployment into a coherent tool for PA transition-risk assessment. This thesis
fills that gap.

\subsection{30$\times$30 and Transition Risk}
\label{sec:lit_transition}

\emph{Transition risk}, in the context of nature-related finance, refers to the financial
exposure arising from policy, regulatory, or market changes enacted in response to
ecological decline \citep{tnfd2023}. The concept originates in the climate-finance
literature, where it describes the risk of stranded assets under carbon pricing and
decarbonisation policy \citep{daumas2024, semieniuk2022, caldecott2021, vanderploeg2020}, and has been extended by nature-related
financial disclosure frameworks to encompass policy-driven land-use change and other
nature-related adjustments as potential sources of financial risk
\citep{govind2025, tnfd2023}.

Reaching the 30$\times$30 target by 2030 requires an unprecedented acceleration of PA
establishment. Achieving 30\%
protection will require not only expanding conventional government-managed PAs but also
incorporating ``Nature's Strongholds'' as large, intact wilderness areas alongside 
governance modalities such as indigenous and community conserved areas and other
effective area-based conservation measures (OECMs) \citep{robinson2024,fitzsimons2025}. 
Dasgupta et al.\ (\citeyear{dasgupta2025}) use country case studies to identify new opportunities for
species protection and emphasise the importance of local conservation stewardship.
More broadly, business-as-usual trends remain insufficient to meet 2030 global
conservation targets \citep{palomo2025}.

The economic consequences of large-scale PA expansion can still be significant
\citep{jago2024}.
Reynaert et al.\ (\citeyear{reynaert2024}) review the evidence on the environmental
impacts of PA policy, especially effects on deforestation and vegetation cover, and
find stronger impacts where land faces pressure of economic development.
Akbari et al.\ (\citeyear{akbari2025}) provide financial-market evidence:
firms operating near newly designated protected areas reduce emissions through
production cutbacks and workforce contraction, with evidence of lower
profitability and market value. These effects are place based: they accrue to
firms, properties, and land uses located in or near areas affected by designation,
while conservation-planning studies emphasise that land-protection costs and
opportunity costs vary spatially and are difficult to measure at fine resolution
\citep{akbari2025, sutton2016, christiaen2025}. The inability to anticipate where
the next wave of PAs will be established therefore creates a location-data problem
for landowners, agricultural producers, firms, and investors with land-linked
exposures \citep{frank2025, sutton2016, christiaen2025, fsb2024, tnfd2023}. Whether this uncertainty constitutes material portfolio-level
transition risk across agriculture, mining, and infrastructure sectors depends on
asset-specific exposure and contract terms; the current evidence base is emerging
\citep{govind2025, tnfd2023}.

Evidence from climate finance shows that spatial environmental risks can be reflected in
asset prices \citep{campiglio2023}. For example, \citet{hino2021} show that disclosing a
property's flood-zone status affects property values, especially where flood risk is salient
to buyers. PA designation risk may operate through a similar channel: once credible
designation probabilities are available, land and asset values may adjust to reflect the
expected restrictions on future land use. Institutions with land-collateralised lending books,
agricultural supply chains, or investments in resource-extraction concessions may therefore
be exposed to PA designation risk \citep{tnfd2023}.
Despite this growing demand for spatially explicit transition-risk inputs, the
literature reviewed here does not provide calibrated, forward-looking probability
maps for near-term PA establishment that can serve as spatial risk inputs for
portfolio screening. This thesis addresses that gap: by training a temporal machine-learning model on historical
PA designation patterns, calibrating its outputs to interpretable probabilities, and
deploying it forward to the 2025--2030 horizon, it provides a spatial risk tool
relevant to nature-related financial disclosure. Demonstrating a full portfolio
aggregation workflow is outside the scope of the thesis; the calibrated risk maps are
a prerequisite input for such applications.

\subsection{Study Areas}
\label{sec:study_areas}

\paragraph{South America:}
South America is selected as the primary study region for three reasons. First, it
lies largely within the Neotropics, the most species-rich terrestrial realm, and spans
major ecological systems including the Amazon rainforest, the Cerrado savanna, the
Andean highlands, and the Atlantic Forest \citep{antonelli2018, ipbesamericas2018}.
This provides a broad range of ecological contexts for model training and evaluation.
Second, protected-area coverage has expanded substantially since
2000, expanding from roughly 15\% to 25.6\% of terrestrial area in the WDPA
rasterisation used here. This thesis-derived panel therefore contains enough
observed designation events to train a transition model.
Third, the
continent shows wide cross-country variation in the governance indicators used as
model predictors \citep{wgi2025, vdem2025}, enabling the model to learn from a broad
range of institutional contexts.

The study covers all sovereign terrestrial land areas of South America on the common
$1 \times 1$~km reference grid as described in Section~\ref{sec:harmonisation}, including
French Guiana (overseas French territory),
but excluding Antarctic territories and open-ocean pixels. Inland water bodies are
included in the raster extent; coverage percentages refer to the total terrestrial
land mask consistent with the WDPA rasterisation boundary. This yields approximately 20.8 million unique grid cells observed annually
from 2000 to 2024 (25 time periods), for a total of approximately 413 million
pixel--year observations after excluding already-protected pixels.

\paragraph{United States:}
The United States is chosen as a robustness check because it provides a structurally
distinct test environment: a federal system, one national governance regime in the
panel, and a low formal WDPA coverage of 7.7\% in the thesis raster baseline.
The US is not a CBD Party and did not formally adopt the Kunming-Montreal GBF.
It pursued a domestic 30$\times$30 goal through the America the Beautiful initiative
\citep{doi2021america}, but the 2025 revocation of Executive Order~14008 made future
federal designation pressure more uncertain over the thesis horizon
\citep{whitehouse2025eo14154}.
This institutional contrast is precisely the point:
the US serves as a robustness test of whether the predictive framework, trained on
a different governance structure and designation process, still recovers
historically coherent risk patterns. The United States also provides a distinctive
public-land setting: the federal government owns roughly 640 million acres
(\(\approx\)28\% of US land), including 244.4 million acres administered by the
Bureau of Land Management and 192.9 million acres administered by the Forest
Service \citep{crs2020federallands}. The observed test-period positive rate of approximately 0.04\% creates a severe
class-imbalance environment for calibration and rare-event detection.

\paragraph{Southeast Asia:}
Southeast Asia is chosen as a second robustness region because its biome composition,
including tropical and subtropical forests, mangroves, and montane ecosystems, shares
several WWF biome classes with South America \citep{olson2001}. It is therefore a
natural test of whether predictive patterns learned in one tropical region transfer to
an ecologically related but institutionally distinct context. At the same time, the
region spans centralised states, archipelagic federations, and post-colonial
conservation institutions across ASEAN member states, plus Timor-Leste and Papua New
Guinea as ecologically contiguous territories included in the raster extent. Its
governance indicators vary substantially across countries \citep{wgi2025, vdem2025},
and its formal WDPA coverage is 14.0\% in the thesis raster baseline. Together, these
properties provide a challenging out-of-sample environment.

\subsection{Variables and Data Sources}

\subsubsection{Target Variable}
\label{sec:target}

Protected-area boundaries and designation dates are obtained from the World Database
on Protected Areas (WDPA; \citealp{wdpa, unepwcmc2026}),
the most comprehensive global PA inventory. WDPA polygons are filtered to
\texttt{STATUS~=~Designated}, terrestrial designation types only, excluding point
records (which carry no areal information), OECMs (counted separately from formal PAs
and excluded from this analysis), and polygons with a missing or zero designation year. 
The resulting polygons are rasterised to the 1~km reference grid: a cell
is classified as protected in year $t$ if more than 50\% of its area overlaps with a
qualifying WDPA polygon whose designation year is $\leq t$. This cumulative definition
ensures that once protected, a cell remains so, consistent with the formal transition
indicator introduced in Section~\ref{sec:methods}.

The WDPA records the initial transition into formal protected status. If a PA is
subsequently de-listed or has its boundaries contracted, a rare event in
the data, the cell retains protected status in our representation. This choice
reflects the focus of the analysis on initial designation rather than subsequent
legal change.
Transition-risk analysis is primarily concerned with the risk of new designation
rather than de-listing, so the cumulative indicator is the appropriate target.

\paragraph{Existing conservation context:}
Distance to the nearest PA boundary (\texttt{dist\_wdpa}) captures spatial path
dependence: designations cluster near the existing network as buffer zones and
corridor links. Lagging by one year prevents the model from exploiting same-year
PA information.

\paragraph{Environmental conditions:}
Annual median NDVI proxies vegetation density; the Hansen deforestation variables
capture habitat extent and land-use pressure; MODIS burned area records fire
disturbance. SRTM elevation and slope control for terrain-driven variation in
accessibility and agricultural suitability. The 19 WorldClim bioclimatic variables
characterise each cell's long-run environmental envelope. Annual MODIS land-cover
class (IGBP scheme) captures broad land-use context. The five GSN layers add
globally standardised conservation-priority signals \citep{dinerstein2020}:
high-biodiversity areas
identify species-richness centres; intact wilderness flags undisturbed ecosystems;
climate-stabilisation areas capture carbon-dense and hydrologically critical zones;
wildlife corridors delineate PA connectivity; and WWF ecoregion boundaries provide
a biogeographic stratification.

\paragraph{Human pressure and economic activity:}
Population density proxies development intensity and the political opportunity cost
of designation. Harmonised DMSP/VIIRS night-time lights reflect economic activity
and built infrastructure. Road network density (GRIP) captures accessibility and
development pressure. Power plant and oil-and-gas infrastructure proxy industrial
capital investment, where extraction interests raise the political cost of
conservation. Agricultural land value provides a direct economic proxy: each
country's annual gross agricultural production value \citep{faostat2024}
is allocated to crop and pasture pixels via MODIS land-cover weights, with cropland
weighted 3$\times$ higher than pasture as a simplifying assumption. This reflects
the higher cropland values reported in USDA land-value data \citep{usdanass2025};
the same ratio is applied outside the US as an approximation, noted as a limitation
in Section~\ref{sec:discussion_limitations}.
This assigns a country-level average value-per-km$^2$ to each agricultural pixel,
capturing commodity price and production variation without parcel-level data.
The variable measures agricultural production value per area, not land
transaction value, and is used as a proxy for the opportunity cost of designation.

\paragraph{Institutional predictors:}
Distance to formally recognised indigenous and community lands (LandMark) captures
the spatial overlap between indigenous territories and conservation expansion, a
substantively important pattern in South America and Southeast Asia. Country-level
governance indicators capture the institutional capacity and political economy of
each country. WGI Government Effectiveness and Rule of Law scores proxy broad state
capacity and institutional quality relevant to conservation implementation. The
V-Dem Liberal Democracy Index
captures the quality of democratic institutions and civil society engagement, which
shapes how responsive conservation policy is to public and NGO pressure. The DPI
executive ideology score (left/centre/right classification) controls for
political-economy variation in conservation priorities across administrations \citep{wangsun2022}.

\paragraph{Static versus dynamic predictors:}
Variables that are time-invariant, such as elevation, slope, WorldClim climatology, road
networks, GSN layers, oil and gas infrastructure, and distance to indigenous territories,
are treated as static and repeated across all years for a given cell. Time-varying
variables, such as NDVI, deforestation, wildfire, population density, night-time lights,
distance to PAs, agricultural land value (annual FAO production), and governance
indicators (WGI, V-Dem, DPI; assigned by country-year) are updated annually. Road and
energy-infrastructure expansion are dynamic in reality but observed here as fixed
snapshots; this approximation is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion_limitations}.

\paragraph{Missing values:}
Missing values arise from genuine data gaps (e.g.\ incomplete satellite coverage in
cloud-persistent regions). Rather than imputing missing values, they are retained as
\texttt{NaN} sentinels and handled natively by the tree models used
here. This accepts measurement gaps rather than fabricating values. For population
density (available at five-year intervals) and night-time lights (a small
end-of-series gap), forward-filling is applied: for any year $t$ without a direct
observation, the most recent prior-year value is carried forward within each cell.
This deterministic last-observation-carried-forward approximation \citep{little2019}
is used only for source layers with coarser temporal release schedules, not for target
labels or model outputs.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Reference grid:}
\label{sec:harmonisation}
Raster alignment, panel construction, and model inference use a regular
$1 \times 1$~km reference grid in EPSG:3857 (WGS~84 Web Mercator). This resolution is
compatible with the main input layers (WDPA, WorldClim, GPW, MODIS) and follows prior
global PA-siting and biodiversity modelling work that uses 1~km or 1~km$^2$ global
grids \citep{joppa2009, tuanmu2015, schuster2023}. It retains landscape-scale
gradients in accessibility, land cover, and existing PA proximity while remaining
computationally tractable for continental panels exceeding 100 million observations.
Web Mercator introduces latitude-dependent scale and area distortion that increases
away from the equator \citep{snyder1987}. Because all predictor layers are
co-registered to the same grid, every
feature within a cell is subject to the same geometric distortion, so within-cell
covariate relationships seen by tree-based models are unaffected.
Distance-based features are slightly overstated at higher
latitudes. All area-based quantities reported in this thesis
(coverage percentages, top-1\%\ zone sizes, agricultural exposure, and 30$\times$30
gap estimates) are computed by reprojecting pixel counts to an equal-area reference
(EPSG:6933 for South America, EPSG:5070 for the US, EPSG:8859 for SE Asia) to correct
for the Mercator areal distortion; the EPSG:3857 grid is used only for feature
alignment, not for area calculations.
Ocean pixels and pixels
outside each continental study boundary are excluded, so the risk set contains only
land cells with complete covariate coverage. Reprojection, resampling, and panel
assembly procedures are described in Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}.

\label{sec:preprocessing}
\paragraph{Reprojection and resampling:}
Every input raster is reprojected to the common 1~km backbone grid and aligned to
calendar years. The reprojection method differs by variable type: bilinear
interpolation is used for continuous variables (elevation, NDVI, climate, population
density, night-time lights), which estimates each new cell as a weighted average of
nearby source cells; nearest-neighbour interpolation is used for categorical and
binary layers (land-cover class, PA status, conservation priority), which copies the
closest source class and therefore avoids mixing categories. For satellite time series with multiple
acquisitions per year, values are collapsed to a single annual summary: the annual
median for continuous variables (reducing cloud artefacts and sensor noise) and the
cumulative sum for fire events (accumulating total burned area over the year).

\paragraph{Panel assembly:}
Following reprojection and temporal alignment, all layers are merged into a unified
pixel--year panel. Each row corresponds to a single grid cell $i$ observed in year $t$
and contains the binary target variable $\text{transition}_{i,t}$ (defined formally
in Section~\ref{sec:framework}), the full set of static and dynamic predictor
variables, and cell and year identifiers. The South America panel contains
approximately 413~million pixel--year observations across 20.8~million unique grid
cells over 25 annual periods (2000--2024); comparable panels are constructed for the
United States and Southeast Asia.

\subsection{Modelling Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\paragraph{Problem formulation:}
The objective is to estimate, for each currently unprotected 1~km$^2$ grid cell $i$
observed in year $t$, the probability that cell $i$ becomes newly designated as a
protected area within the next five years. Let $y_{i,t} \in \{0,1\}$ denote
whether cell $i$ is protected in year $t$. The target variable is a five-year forward-looking binary indicator:
\begin{equation}
  \text{transition}_{i,t} =
  \begin{cases}
    1 & \text{if } y_{i,t} = 0 \text{ and } y_{i,t+k} = 1
        \text{ for any } k \in \{1,\dots,5\}, \\
    0 & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
A pixel receives a positive label in year $t$ if it is currently unprotected and
becomes protected within the next five years. Because the same designation event
creates positive labels across multiple consecutive years (e.g.\ a cell protected in
2015 is labelled positive at $t = 2010, \ldots, 2014$), the dataset contains more
positive rows than unique designation events. All labels look strictly forward from
year $t$, so no future information leaks into the feature vector.

A five-year horizon is chosen for three reasons: it aligns directly with the
2025--2030 window of the 30$\times$30 target; it absorbs administrative recording
delays of up to three years in the WDPA without censoring true designation events
\citep{bingham2019}; and it provides a large enough positive class per year to
support reliable model training despite rare events.

\paragraph{Risk set and right-censoring:}
Only unprotected pixels ($y_{i,t} = 0$) are eligible for the transition and therefore
included in the analysis. Once a pixel becomes protected, it exits the risk set
permanently and does not contribute further observations. This prevents the model from
learning to predict non-events for land that is already protected.

Right-censoring is a technical constraint arising from the finite data horizon.
To observe the full five-year outcome window, the last year for which a label can be
constructed is 2019 (using PA designations through 2024). All pixel--year observations
beyond 2019 are excluded from model training and evaluation; the target variable is
undefined for them because we cannot verify whether a transition occurs in the full
subsequent five-year window. This cutoff year is denoted \texttt{LAST\_LABEL\_YEAR}~$= 2019$.

\paragraph{Rare-event structure:}
Transitions into protected status are rare: fewer than 0.5\% of eligible pixel--year
observations record a positive outcome. This extreme class imbalance is not an
artefact of data collection but reflects the true pace of PA expansion relative to
the vast unprotected land area. Standard classifiers trained on such data tend to
predict the majority class (non-transition) for almost all observations and achieve
high nominal accuracy while being useless for identifying future designations
\citep{davis2006}. The modelling and evaluation choices described in the following
sections account for this imbalance.

\paragraph{Relation to discrete-time hazard models:}
The panel formulation of Equation~(\ref{eq:target}) is conceptually related to
discrete-time hazard (or duration) models used in econometrics to study the timing
of events such as unemployment spells, technology adoption, or firm bankruptcy
\citep{allison1982, singer1993}.
In the hazard interpretation, each unprotected pixel is ``at risk'' of designation
in each year, and the model estimates the conditional probability of transitioning
given that no transition has occurred yet. The target is a five-year cumulative transition indicator
with overlapping windows; output probabilities should be interpreted as five-year
cumulative designation risks, not annual conditional hazards.

\paragraph{Prediction, not prescription:}
The framework learns where governments have historically designated PAs and
extrapolates this pattern forward. It does not predict where PAs \emph{should} be
designated for maximum conservation impact. The two questions have different answers because real designation is not only shaped
by biodiversity optimisation, but also by political feasibility and opportunity cost \citep{joppa2009, venter2018}.

\subsection{Feature Engineering}
\label{sec:features}

Raw cell-level features describe conditions at a single pixel. Because PA designation
is influenced by conditions in the surrounding landscape, additional features are engineered to encode spatial
context at multiple scales.

\paragraph{Multi-scale spatial smoothing:}
For a selected subset of time-varying predictors (NDVI, population density, night-time
lights, deforestation intensity, and wildfire), uniform averaging kernels of size
$4 \times 4$, $16 \times 16$, and $64 \times 64$ grid cells are applied, corresponding
to neighbourhood extents of 4~km, 16~km, and 64~km respectively. For example, the
16~km smoothed NDVI feature is the arithmetic mean of NDVI over the surrounding
$16 \times 16$ cell window; the model receives this neighbourhood value alongside the
raw cell value. These three scales are designed to capture: (i) immediate local context (4~km: adjacent land use, buffer
zones); (ii) sub-regional landscape dynamics (16~km: agricultural frontier gradients,
infrastructure corridors); and (iii) landscape-scale patterns (64~km: macro-regional
biome boundaries, large-scale deforestation fronts). Including all three scales allows
the model to select the most informative spatial context for each combination of
predictor and location without imposing a single fixed neighbourhood size
\citep{collart2025, mcgarigal2016}.

\paragraph{Distance-based features:}
In addition to smoothed averages, Euclidean distances to the nearest PA boundary,
road segment, power plant, and oil and gas installation are included as features.
Distances are computed in EPSG:3857 and therefore slightly overstate ground distances
at higher latitudes; for South America and Southeast Asia (predominantly within
$\pm 30^\circ$) the overstatement is below 15\%, which does not affect the rank
ordering used by tree splits.
These distance measures directly encode proximity to conservation and development
infrastructure, capturing processes such as incremental PA network expansion and
the expected hindering effect of industrial activity on designation.

\paragraph{Temporal lag:}
All time-varying features, including the distance to existing PAs, are lagged by
one year: features at year $t$ reflect conditions recorded through end of year $t-1$.
This ensures that the model cannot use information about PAs designated in year $t$
itself to predict the transition target for year $t$, which would constitute temporal
leakage. For example, distance to the nearest PA at year $t$ is computed using only
the PA network as it stood at end of year $t-1$, so designation events in year $t$ do
not inform the features used to predict those same events.

\subsection{Models and Calibration}
\label{sec:models}

Three modelling approaches are estimated for each region, in increasing order of
complexity.

\paragraph{Static Suitability Baseline:}
A cross-sectional suitability model trained on year-2000 protection status was
estimated but is excluded from the main results. Because it conflates existing
protection status with future designation likelihood and ignores temporal dynamics
entirely, its scores on unprotected pixels reflect geographic similarity to
already-protected land rather than genuine transition risk --- a fundamentally
different quantity. The panel models described below are better suited to the
forecasting task.

\paragraph{Random Forest:}
A Random Forest \citep{breiman2001} is estimated as a robust bagged-tree benchmark
that can capture non-linear feature interactions without imposing functional-form
assumptions. Class imbalance is handled with
\texttt{class\_weight="balanced\_subsample"}, which upweights rare transition
observations within each bootstrap sample.
Random Forest is reported as the primary model because it performs the best on the on the
policy-relevant metrics (PR-AUC, Precision@1\%, and Brier score) across all three
regions.

\paragraph{LightGBM:}
Light Gradient-Boosting Machine (LightGBM; \citealp{ke2017}) is estimated alongside
Random Forest using the same splits, features, and calibration procedure. LightGBM is
well suited to the 100--350 million row panels because it implements efficient
gradient-boosted decision trees. Class imbalance is handled via
\texttt{scale\_pos\_weight}, set equal to the ratio of negative to positive
observations:
\begin{equation}
  \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} = \frac{N_{\text{neg}}}{N_{\text{pos}}}.
  \label{eq:spw}
\end{equation}
This increases the influence of each positive (transition) observation in the loss
function in proportion to its rarity. The parameter is recomputed from the training
data for each model run. For both panel models, no oversampling or undersampling is
applied; the empirical spatial and temporal structure of the risk set is preserved
\citep{elkan2001}.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter tuning:}
Hyperparameters are selected via Bayesian optimisation using Optuna
\citep{akiba2019}. Conceptually, tuning is a sequence of small historical forecasting
exercises: Optuna proposes one candidate set of model settings (for example tree
depth, leaf size, learning rate, or regularisation strength), the model is trained on
earlier years, and predictive performance is evaluated on the next validation block.
The optimisation objective is PR-AUC (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}), so the chosen
settings are those that best rank rare future designation events in validation data.
The search is run on the 2001--2016 pre-test training artefact using rolling temporal
folds. LightGBM uses 40 trials per region and Random Forest uses 10 trials per
region. Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams} summarises which key parameters are fixed or
tuned; region-specific selected values are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparams}.

\paragraph{Probability Calibration:}
\label{sec:calibration}

Raw model outputs are scores, not probabilities: a score of 0.7 indicates that the
pixel ranks above most others, but does not mean its true designation probability is
70\%. Class-weighting inflates these raw scores relative to true probabilities, so
direct interpretation would be misleading.

To correct this, Platt scaling \citep{platt1999, niculescumizil2005} is applied: a
logistic regression is fitted on out-of-fold model scores to map them to probabilities
that match observed transition rates. In practical terms, if a group of pixels receives
high raw scores but transitions less often than those scores imply, the calibrator
shrinks the scores toward the observed frequency. Because this fitting uses
out-of-fold predictions, no test-set information enters the calibration step.
Reliability diagrams (Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}) provide a visual check of
calibration quality; isotonic regression \citep{zadrozny2002} is computed as a
robustness check. All probability maps and risk scores in this thesis use
Platt-calibrated predictions.

\subsection{Training and Test Design}
\label{sec:splits}

\paragraph{Temporal splits:}
Model training and evaluation strictly preserve temporal order to ensure that all
reported metrics reflect the model's ability to forecast \emph{future} designations
using only \emph{past} information. Year 2000 is excluded from all splits: the WDPA
records it as a cross-sectional baseline in which all pre-existing PAs appear as
newly designated, not as true annual events.

The pre-test labelled window spans 2001--2016. Within this window, earlier years are
used to tune hyperparameters and generate out-of-fold validation predictions for
calibration. These validation predictions are not reported as final model performance.
After hyperparameters and calibrators have been fixed, the final evaluation models are
trained on the full pre-test labelled window and then scored on the held-out future
period.

\paragraph{Train period:}
Using the selected hyperparameters, both Random Forest and LightGBM are trained on
the full labelled pre-test period (2001--2016). The final LightGBM model is trained
without a validation set and without early stopping; its number of boosting rounds is
the \texttt{n\_estimators} value selected during Optuna tuning and stored in the
region-specific parameter file. The final Random Forest analogously uses the
Optuna-selected number of trees and tree-structure parameters. For both models, class
imbalance parameters are recomputed or applied on the final training data rather than
estimated from the test period.

\paragraph{Test period:}
The temporal test set covers 2017--2019. The upper boundary 2019 coincides with
\texttt{LAST\_LABEL\_YEAR}: all pixel--year observations from 2020 onwards are
right-censored because their five-year lookahead labels are incomplete. Every metric
reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} is computed on this future period. The temporal
test is future-out-of-sample but not location-out-of-sample: the same pixel (once it
exits the risk set upon designation) can appear in both training and test years.
Static covariates (elevation, climate, GSN layers) are identical across years for the
same pixel, which could contribute to optimistic performance estimates. The LOBO
evaluation addresses this by withholding entire biomes, providing a stricter spatial
generalisation test.

\paragraph{Leakage prevention:}
Multiple safeguards prevent future information from reaching the model. (i) All
time-varying features are lagged by one year, so the covariate vector at year $t$
reflects conditions recorded through end of year $t-1$ (Section~\ref{sec:features}).
(ii) Distance to existing PAs is computed using only the PA network as of year $t-1$,
so same-year designation events cannot leak into the distance feature for that year.
(iii) Hyperparameters are tuned using the Optuna HPO procedure described above on the
pre-test training artefact, not on the final scored test set. (iv) Probability
calibrators are fitted on out-of-fold validation predictions (temporal-CV predictions
for LightGBM; 2014--2016 earlystop predictions for Random Forest), not on test
predictions. The five-year lookahead window creates overlapping labels across adjacent years, which
may render temporal-test metrics overly optimistic; this is discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion_limitations}.

\paragraph{Temporal sample weighting:}
Conservation-policy dynamics evolve over time, and recent designation patterns are
more informative for forecasting the near future than patterns from two decades ago
\citep{gama2014}.
In the LightGBM training path, observations are therefore assigned a linearly
increasing sample weight: year 2001 receives weight 0.5 and the last training year
receives weight 1.0, with all intermediate years interpolated proportionally. This
places greater emphasis on recent patterns while retaining the full historical record.
Random Forest is trained without explicit temporal sample weights; temporal weighting
is therefore not part of the primary RF specification. The 0.5 floor is a modelling
choice; sensitivity to this value has not been formally tested.

\paragraph{Reproducibility:}
All random components (tree construction, bootstrap sampling, Optuna trial
initialisation) are controlled via a fixed random seed. The full
pipeline is implemented in Python~3.12 using scikit-learn~1.4, LightGBM~4.3
\citep{ke2017,pedregosa2011}, and GeoPandas~0.14, with intermediate outputs stored in
Apache Parquet format for efficient column-oriented access. Panels of 100--350 million rows exceed available memory, so all pipeline
stages use batch-streaming I/O; model training is executed on the ETH Zurich Euler
high-performance computing cluster. Jobs are submitted as SLURM array tasks.
Key library versions, data release dates, and SLURM job templates are recorded in the
repository's \texttt{environment.yml} and \texttt{data\_provenance.csv}.
The full pipeline code, including preprocessing, training, and evaluation scripts,
is maintained in a version-controlled repository; together with the fixed seed,
recorded package versions, and data provenance files, it constitutes the
reproducibility artefact for the main evaluation results.

\subsection{Evaluation} 
\label{sec:metrics}

Because the positive class constitutes fewer than 0.5\% of observations, accuracy-based
metrics (e.g.\ overall accuracy, $F_1$-score) are uninformative: a model that predicts
zero transitions everywhere achieves $> 99.5\%$ accuracy \citep{davis2006}. Evaluation
therefore focuses on metrics that are robust to severe class imbalance and that
directly reflect the ranking quality of predicted risks.

\paragraph{Precision--Recall AUC (PR-AUC):}
The primary metric. The precision--recall curve plots precision (fraction of top-ranked
pixels that actually transition) against recall (fraction of all transitions captured)
as the classification threshold is varied:
\begin{align}
  \text{Precision}(k) &= \frac{TP(k)}{TP(k) + FP(k)}, \quad
  \text{Recall}(k) = \frac{TP(k)}{TP(k) + FN(k)}.
\end{align}
Here $TP(k)$, $FP(k)$, and $FN(k)$ denote true positives, false positives, and false
negatives at threshold $k$. PR-AUC is computed as scikit-learn's \texttt{average\_precision\_score}, which uses
the step-function approximation.
Under severe class imbalance, PR-AUC is a more informative summary of discriminative
performance than ROC-AUC because it places weight on the model's ability to correctly
rank positive observations at the top of the score distribution \citep{davis2006}.

\paragraph{ROC-AUC:}
ROC-AUC measures the probability that a randomly chosen positive observation receives
a higher predicted score than a randomly chosen negative. A value of 0.5 corresponds
to random ranking and 1.0 to perfect discrimination. Although ROC-AUC is less
sensitive than PR-AUC under extreme class imbalance, since it is dominated by the large
number of true negatives; it provides a useful reference point for comparing model
quality across settings with different base rates and for detecting degenerate or
anti-predictive models.

\paragraph{Precision at top-$k$\% and lift:}
For policy use, the most relevant question could be: ``if I inspect the top $k$\% of
predicted-risk pixels, what fraction will actually be designated?'' This is precision
at top $k$\% of the score distribution ($P@k\%$). To account for differences in
base rates across evaluation splits, we also report \emph{lift}:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Lift}@k\% = \frac{P@k\%}{\text{baseline positive rate}}.
\end{equation}
A lift of 60 at the top 1\% means that screening the top 1\% of pixels yields 60
times more future designations per unit area than random selection. The main
screening threshold is $k = 1\%$, matching the risk maps and exposure analysis.
Precision@0.1\% and Lift@0.1\%, as well as Precision@5\% and Lift@5\%, are 
additionally reported as upper-tail diagnostics.

\paragraph{Brier score:}
The Brier score \citep{brier1950} is the mean squared error of calibrated predicted
probabilities:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Brier} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i,t} \bigl(\hat{p}_{i,t} - y_{i,t}\bigr)^2.
\end{equation}
Lower values are better. A naive model that always predicts the base rate $\pi$
achieves a score of $\pi(1 - \pi)$; any useful model should beat this no-information
baseline. For South America ($\pi \approx 0.008$), the no-information baseline is
$\approx 0.0079$; per-region baselines are reported alongside model scores in
Table~\ref{tab:sa_metrics}.

\paragraph{Expected Calibration Error (ECE):}
Expected Calibration Error (ECE) \citep{guo2017} measures how well predicted
probabilities match observed transition rates.
Predictions are grouped into equal-width bins; within each bin, the mean predicted
probability $\overline{p}_b$ is compared to the actual fraction of transitions
$\overline{y}_b$:
\begin{equation}
  \text{ECE} = \sum_{b=1}^{B} \frac{|n_b|}{N}
    \left| \overline{p}_b - \overline{y}_b \right|,
\end{equation}
where $n_b$ is the bin size. A well-calibrated model has low ECE: a predicted
probability of 5\% should correspond to an observed rate near 5\%. ECE is reported
before and after Platt scaling to quantify the improvement from calibration.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Feature Importance (SHAP):}
\label{sec:shap_methods}

To interpret which features drive predictions, SHapley Additive exPlanations
(SHAP; \citealp{lundberg2017}) are computed on the held-out test set (2017--2019).
For each observation, SHAP assigns every feature a contribution score: a positive
value means the feature raises the predicted risk above the average; a negative value
means it lowers it. Averaging absolute SHAP values across all test observations
produces a global feature ranking that reflects genuine out-of-sample model behaviour.
Unless otherwise stated, reported SHAP rankings use the Random Forest, the primary
model for transition-risk analysis.

\subsection{Spatial Robustness Evaluation}
\label{sec:spatial_cv}

In addition to temporal out-of-sample evaluation, spatial robustness is assessed
through a three-layer framework that tests whether the model's predictive patterns
hold across geographic settings it was not explicitly trained on \citep{ploton2020}.

\subsubsection{Leave-One-Biome-Out (LOBO)}
\label{sec:lobo}
LOBO cross-validation, adapted from spatial leave-one-group-out procedures
\citep{ploton2020, roberts2017}, withholds entire biomes rather than future time
periods. Biomes are defined using the WWF terrestrial ecoregion classification from
the Global Safety Net (GSN) layer. For each biome $b$, the model is trained on all
other biomes and evaluated on held-out test-period pixels from biome $b$. Low LOBO
performance therefore indicates that designation patterns in the held-out biome are
not well represented by the rest of the continent.

PR-AUC is the primary LOBO metric because it is more informative than ROC-AUC under
extreme class imbalance. Since random-classifier PR-AUC equals the positive rate,
raw PR-AUC values are not directly comparable across biomes with different
designation frequencies. Results are therefore also reported as \emph{PR-AUC lift}:
observed PR-AUC divided by the biome positive rate. A lift of~1 corresponds to random
performance; values above~1 indicate predictive signal above the naive baseline.
Raw PR-AUC values are reported alongside lift in Appendix~\ref{app:lobo} for
completeness.

\subsubsection{Per-Biome Performance}
\label{sec:cv2}
Here, the same fully trained model is evaluated separately for each biome. Unlike
LOBO, no biome is withheld during training. This shows where the model works well
versus where it struggles, even when the biome's own history was available for
training. It provides a geographic map of predictive reliability, useful for
communicating where risk scores can be trusted. 

\subsubsection{Cross-Continental Transfer}
\label{sec:cv3}
Cross-continental transfer provides the most demanding spatial test: a model trained
on one region is scored on another without any retraining. Transfer is tested in
all six directions between South America (SA), the United States (USA), and Southeast
Asia (SEA), i.e.\ SA$\to$USA, USA$\to$SA, SA$\to$SEA, USA$\to$SEA, SEA$\to$SA,
and SEA$\to$USA, using the feature set common to all three regions. Strong transfer
performance would suggest the model has learned universal drivers of PA designation.
Near-random performance would indicate that designation patterns are region-specific,
although differences in feature distributions across continents also contribute to
poor transfer.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Forward Prediction to 2030}
\label{sec:forward}

\paragraph{Deployment model:}
The forward prediction setup is identical to the evaluation model in every respect,
using the same hyperparameters, same features, same class-weighting scheme, and same
Platt calibration procedure, with one difference: the training window is extended
to include all non-censored years. A deployment model is therefore trained on
2001--2019 (all years for which the five-year transition target can be constructed
without right-censoring), using the same hyperparameter configuration selected by
the Optuna HPO stage and the same number of boosting rounds as the evaluation model.
Because the deployment model trains on all labeled data including the 2017--2019
test period, no held-out set is available for calibration. An auxiliary model trained
on 2001--2016 is therefore used to score 2017--2019 as a held-out proxy, and the
resulting out-of-fold predictions are used to fit the Platt and isotonic calibrators.
This is a practical approximation: the calibrator is fitted on the 2017--2019 proxy
period rather than as a true out-of-fold estimate for the 2001--2019 deployment model.
The proxy period immediately precedes deployment, and the walk-forward backtest
(Appendix~\ref{app:backtest}) provides a conservative check on deployment-style
ranking performance; it cannot fully verify absolute calibration for 2025--2030.

\paragraph{Forward inference:}
Forward prediction treats 2024 as the forecast origin. Because time-varying predictors
are lagged by one year, the model uses information available through end-2023 and
scores all pixels still unprotected at that date. Each score is interpreted as the
calibrated probability that the pixel enters the PA network over the next five years,
which is the period relevant for assessing progress toward the 30$\times$30 target.

\paragraph{Policy scenarios:}
Three designation scenarios are constructed, each differing
in the total new area designated:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Business-as-usual (BAU):} new area equals the observed
    2019--2024 designation volume, projecting recent rates forward.
  \item \textbf{Moderate:} new area targets the midpoint between each
    region's current coverage and 30\%.
  \item \textbf{30$\times$30:} new area targets full 30\% terrestrial
    coverage by 2030.
\end{itemize}
Under each scenario, pixels are ranked by probability and the top-ranking pixels are
selected until the area target is met. These maps show where designation is most
likely based on historical patterns. Hotspot clusters are
identified via spatial clustering \citep{ester1996} on the top-probability pixels.

\paragraph{Backtesting:}
To verify that the forward procedure works as intended, it is replicated at four
historical origins: $T \in \{2013, 2015, 2017, 2019\}$. For each origin, the model is
trained only on years whose five-year lookahead is fully observed, scores pixels
unprotected in year $T$, and is evaluated against realised designations in
$T{+}1$--$T{+}5$. The closest analogue to the 2024 deployment setting is
$T=2019$: the model is trained on 2001--2014, scores unprotected pixels in 2019,
and is evaluated against 2020--2024 designations. Full backtest results are reported
in Appendix~\ref{app:backtest}.

\subsection{South America: Model Performance}
\label{sec:results_sa}

The South America temporal test set covers the 2017--2019 prediction years and
contains approximately 47.7~million eligible pixel--year observations. Within this
risk set, 380,064 observations receive a positive five-year designation label,
corresponding to a baseline positive rate of 0.797\%. This class imbalance provides
the reference point for interpreting precision--recall metrics and Lift@K: a random
classifier would recover positives at approximately the same 0.8\% rate. For
comparison, the corresponding baseline positive rates are approximately 0.04\% in
the United States and 0.6\% in Southeast Asia, so precision--recall metrics are
interpreted relative to each region's own class imbalance.

\subsubsection{Discrimination Metrics}

Table~\ref{tab:sa_metrics} reports performance for the two tree-based models used

summarises this ranking over all positives and negatives, while PR-AUC, Precision@K,

\subsubsection{Event-Level Coverage}
\label{sec:results_sa_events}

Pixel--year metrics such as PR-AUC penalise any error in timing: a model that
correctly identifies the \emph{location} of a future designation but places it one
year off still receives no credit for that positive. For a policy application such as
screening land for transition risk, the question of interest might be spatial rather than
temporal: does the model identify the right \emph{places}, regardless of precisely
when designation occurs within the next five years?

Event-level coverage answers this question directly: each unique pixel that transitions
within any test-year's five-year prediction window is counted once, and the metric
checks whether it falls within the top-1\% risk zone. Under this aggregation,
the Random Forest achieves an event-level Recall@1\% of 69.1\% and a
Precision@1\% of 97.0\%: within the historical test window, more than two-thirds of
unique PA establishment events fell inside just 1\% of eligible land, and almost all
pixels in that top-risk zone later received formal designation. The LightGBM achieves
an event-level Recall@1\% of 60.3\% and Precision@1\% of 84.7\%. This is the
sharpest targeting result in the analysis, but it should be read as evidence of
high-precision spatial localisation over a five-year window, not as parcel-level
certainty.

\subsubsection{Probability Calibration}
\label{sec:results_sa_calibration}

Raw model scores are systematically over-confident before Platt scaling. After
calibration, the RF ECE is 0.0116 and the LightGBM ECE is 0.00569. Because
high-score bins contain few observations, calibration in the upper tail is assessed
from the reliability diagrams (Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}) alongside ECE. This
presents a tradeoff: RF delivers superior precision-recall performance at the cost of
moderately higher calibration error than LGBM; for applications requiring tighter
absolute probability estimates, LGBM may be preferable. The key result is that Platt
scaling converts raw scores into empirically calibrated five-year risk estimates,
making the maps usable for portfolio screening rather than only for ordinal ranking.

\paragraph{Accessibility and settlement pressure:} Road distance (\texttt{dist\_road}) is rank~1,

Smoothed density (\texttt{GPW\_\allowbreak smooth16}) ranks third ($0.035$). Night-time

suppressed from the highest-risk tier. Road distance separates frontier or interior

settlement density, while \texttt{GPW\_\allowbreak smooth16}
captures nearby settlement intensity in the surrounding landscape. Night-time lights

not fully capture infrastructure and market access.

\paragraph{Opportunity cost and land cover:} Land cover (rank~4; mean $|\text{SHAP}|{=}0.035$)

economic feasibility of protection rather than ecological value alone. The categorical land-cover
feature lets the model distinguish forest, shrubland, grassland, cropland, and bare
land regimes, while \texttt{economic\_value\_\allowbreak lag1} adds an explicit agricultural rent
proxy. Their joint ranking shows that both observed land use and expected productive
value help separate likely designation areas from higher-conflict land.

\paragraph{Ecological and biome context:} Dry-season precipitation
(\texttt{WorldClim\_b14}, rank~5; mean $|\text{SHAP}|{=}0.034$) and
biodiversity-priority features (\texttt{GSN\_b2\_smooth16}, rank~9; $0.027$;
\texttt{GSN\_b2}, rank~12; $0.023$) also enter the upper ranking, but below the
main accessibility and land-use variables. Dry-season
precipitation should be read mainly as a biome and regional-climate proxy, separating
Amazonian, Cerrado, Andean, and southern temperate designation environments. The GSN
variables identify biodiversity-priority pixels both locally and in a 16~km
neighbourhood, indicating that ecological priority contributes to the score, but is
filtered through the accessibility and land-use conditions above.

\subsubsection{Leave-One-Biome-Out (LOBO)}

The LOBO evaluation tests whether patterns learned on other biomes generalise to a

The transfer matrix is less a shortcut to global risk maps than a diagnostic of where

\subsubsection{Per-Biome Performance}
\label{sec:results_biome_strat}

Biome-stratified metrics reveal where risk scores are most and least actionable
(Figure~\ref{fig:biome_breakdown_sa}). PR-AUC varies substantially across SA biomes
($0.17$--$0.90$), far more than ROC-AUC ($0.56$--$1.00$), reflecting the greater
sensitivity of precision-recall performance to local designation rates.
Temperate Broadleaf \& Mixed Forests offers the most operationally useful profile:
PR-AUC of $0.87$ combined with a high positive rate (7.8\%) and Lift@1\% of
$12.8\times$, meaning top-ranked pixels in this biome capture a meaningful absolute
share of observed transitions.
Temperate Grasslands \& Savannas achieves the highest PR-AUC ($0.90$) and Lift@1\%
($99.9\times$), yet its near-zero positive rate ($\approx$0.09\%) means that even
highly ranked pixels rarely transition in absolute terms.
Mediterranean Forests, with PR-AUC of $0.17$, are the hardest to predict even
with the biome's own history available.

The Flooded Grasslands comparison with the LOBO results is instructive: the biome
achieves PR-AUC of $0.75$ within-sample, yet is the only biome with below-random
LOBO lift. Together, the two results indicate that designation follows a coherent but
unique pattern, learnable from its own history, but not transferable from
neighbouring biomes.

\subsection{Regional Results}
\label{sec:results_regional}

Results for the United States and Southeast Asia extend the framework to structurally
distinct institutional contexts; the US result is dominated by a geographic
concentration artefact that inflates apparent performance, while Southeast Asia shows
strong genuine discrimination across both algorithms.

\subsubsection{United States}
\label{sec:results_usa}
The Random Forest achieves a near-perfect ROC-AUC of 0.999 and a Lift@1\% of
$99.6\times$, but these metrics reflect a geographic concentration artefact rather
than genuine generalisation: approximately 85\% of all test-period US PA
establishments fall within the Deserts and Xeric Shrublands biome (Bureau of Land
Management public land in the American West), which is spatially compact and readily
separable from surrounding land. The RF effectively memorises the proximity of a
small number of events, inflating apparent discrimination. The LightGBM ROC-AUC of
0.707 and Lift@1\% of $55.0\times$ provide a more conservative and less confounded
estimate; PR-AUC is 0.538 (RF) and 0.015 (LGBM), the large divergence reflecting
the extreme rarity of the positive class (0.040\%) and its geographic concentration.
The extreme class imbalance ($\approx 0.04\%$ positive rate, twenty times rarer than
South America) means even strong Lift values coincide with low absolute precision: in
the RF the top-0.1\% of pixel--years has precision 0.351 and captures 87.3\% of all
positive labels, while the top-1\% zone captures 99.6\% but precision falls to 0.040
as the screened set dwarfs observed events. Event-level Precision@1\% is 6.0\% (RF)
and 2.6\% (LGBM). US forward projections should therefore be interpreted as spatial
risk \emph{rankings} rather than calibrated absolute probabilities.

The LOBO analysis reveals near-zero absolute generalisation across US biomes (mean
PR-AUC $\approx 0.001$; three of five evaluated folds at or below lift~$= 1$; median
fold lift $0.8\times$), consistent with the BLM federal-land concentration: without
parcel-level ownership data the signal is not transferable across ecological contexts
(Table~\ref{tab:lobo_usa}).

Full metrics, LOBO results (Table~\ref{tab:lobo_usa}, Figure~\ref{fig:lobo_usa}),
and probability maps (Figure~\ref{fig:probmap_usa}) are in
Appendices~\ref{app:lobo}--\ref{app:supp_figures}.

\paragraph{Feature Importance (SHAP).}
The US RF SHAP analysis reveals a driver profile dominated by opportunity cost,
protected-area network geography, and terrain. Agricultural land value
(\texttt{economic\_value\_lag1}, rank~1; mean SHAP $-0.048$) is the leading
predictor, indicating that higher-value working land is suppressed from the
high-risk tier. Distance to the existing PA network (\texttt{dist\_wdpa}, rank~2)
and smoothed elevation features (ranks~3--5) capture the western federal-land
geography in which new designations concentrate. Population density and night-time
lights remain negative predictors (country-level governance indicators add little
within-country spatial variation and should be read cautiously).
The corresponding RF SHAP plot is reported in Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:shap_rf_appendix}.

\subsubsection{Southeast Asia}
\label{sec:results_seasia}

The Southeast Asia Random Forest achieves a ROC-AUC of 0.987 and a PR-AUC of 0.571;
LightGBM achieves ROC-AUC 0.980 and PR-AUC 0.538. The RF Lift@1\% is $82.6\times$
(LGBM: $74.5\times$), confirming strong discrimination across both algorithms.
Upper-tail precision is also high: the RF reaches Precision@0.1\% of 0.780, and
LightGBM reaches 0.823. At the event level, the RF captures 65.0\% of unique
designated pixels in the top 1\% at a precision of 91.9\% (LGBM: 67.5\% recall,
95.5\% precision). New PAs are
predominantly located in forested upland areas of Borneo, Sumatra, peninsular
Malaysia, and the Philippines, which differ sharply from surrounding lowland
agricultural zones. The geographic concentration of designations means that elevation
is a strong structural predictor; nonetheless, the full multi-feature model adds
substantive discrimination over a simple elevation threshold, as the SHAP analysis
below illustrates. A small number of large PA polygons in Borneo may drive a
disproportionate share of positive observations.

The LOBO analysis yields a mean PR-AUC of 0.204 across five evaluable biomes
(Table~\ref{tab:lobo_seasia}), with all five achieving above-random Lift@1\%,
indicating that learned designation patterns transfer across ecological contexts within
the region. The two largest folds by number of positives --- Tropical \& Subtropical
Moist Broadleaf Forests (51,475 positives, Lift@1\% $5.6\times$) and Dry Broadleaf
Forests (17,702 positives, $13.0\times$) --- provide the most robust evidence of
within-region transferability; smaller folds show higher lift but are more sensitive
to near-zero positive rates. Three biome folds with zero test-period designations are
excluded from the mean.

\paragraph{Feature Importance (SHAP).}
The Southeast Asia RF SHAP analysis shows a more mixed institutional, economic, and
biophysical profile. Policy variables occupy three of the top four ranks, alongside
agricultural land value at rank~2, but their signed effects are heterogeneous and
should not be read causally. Distance to indigenous lands, population density, land
cover, climate variables, and elevation also rank highly, indicating that Southeast
Asian designation risk is not simply an elevation screen: the RF combines governance
context with low-conflict upland and forest-frontier geography.
The corresponding RF SHAP plot is reported in Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:shap_rf_appendix}.

Full metrics, LOBO results (Table~\ref{tab:lobo_seasia}), and probability maps
(Figure~\ref{fig:probmap_seasia}) are in Appendices~\ref{app:lobo}--\ref{app:supp_figures}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Designation Drivers}
\label{sec:disc_drivers}
The SHAP evidence reported above points to a compact
political-economy interpretation: PA designation is most likely where ecological or
biome signals coincide with low-conflict land-use conditions. The relative importance
of these mechanisms differs across regions: accessibility and settlement pressure
dominate South America, federal land and terrain geography dominate the United States,
and institutional context interacts with upland forest-frontier geography in Southeast
Asia, but the common pattern is that economically active land is systematically
suppressed from the high-risk tier.

This matters for 30$\times$30 implementation. If future designations follow historical
logic, additional coverage will not simply flow to the most underprotected biodiversity
or climate-stabilisation areas; it will concentrate where conservation is politically
and economically feasible. Countries with weak administrative capacity may fail to
protect ecologically valuable land, while countries with strong institutions may still
avoid high-opportunity-cost landscapes unless compensation mechanisms, conservation
easements, carbon-market incentives, or binding ecological representation targets alter
the payoff structure \citep{venter2018, eckert2023}.

The result aligns with the socio-environmental siting logic in \citet{mouillot2024},
but extends it from cross-sectional classification of existing PAs to forward-looking
transition prediction. Because directly comparable PA-transition benchmarks are scarce,
the reported ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, and Precision@$K$\% metrics should be read as an initial
benchmark for this specific task rather than as a direct ranking against adjacent
deforestation or regulatory-jurisdiction models \citep{mayfield2017, ball2022,
greenhill2024}.

\subsection{Biome Coverage Gaps}
\label{sec:disc_representation}
The LOBO analysis reveals a predictable pattern of biome underrepresentation under
historical dynamics. Biomes with high LOBO performance are those where designation
follows consistent geographic gradients: in South America, temperate and tropical
forests with distinctive elevation and remoteness signatures generalise well across
biome boundaries, while certain tropical dry biomes achieve high lift owing to very
low positive rates. Biomes with poor LOBO performance exhibit established
designation patterns driven by institutional and political factors: temperate conifer
forests in the US and flooded grasslands and tropical savannas in South America show
near-random or below-random lift, indicating that the static geospatial feature set
cannot capture the seasonal, tenure-based, or agreement-driven processes that govern
designation in those ecosystems.

The GSN alignment analysis (Section~\ref{sec:forward_overlap}) confirms this
pattern: only 27.2\% of projected 30$\times$30 designations overlap with biodiversity
priority zones. Even if 30\% terrestrial coverage is achieved, the distribution
across biomes is likely to remain biased toward ecologically ``easy'' land. Closing
this gap would require policy choices that redirect protection toward
underrepresented ecosystems, for example through conservation finance instruments,
direct payments, or binding biome-specific targets.

\subsection{Regional Transferability}
\label{sec:disc_transfer}
Cross-continental transfer performance varies markedly by source--target corridor
and algorithm (full results in Table~\ref{tab:transfer_full},
Appendix~\ref{app:transfer}). LGBM transfer is highly asymmetric: it is strong from
South America to Southeast Asia (ROC-AUC 0.796) and moderate from the United States
to South America (0.617), but near-random or below-random in the remaining four
directions (0.287--0.524). RF is more stable across corridors, remaining near or
moderately above random in all directions (0.514--0.639), but it does not match the
best LGBM transfer case.
Protected-area designation is institutionally specific: its spatial pattern
is not a universal law derivable from physical geography but a product of specific
national legal frameworks, political histories, and institutional capacities.
Even a feature set that includes both remotely-sensed covariates and country-level
governance indicators cannot capture the specific congressional acts that create
a national monument in Utah or the presidential decrees that establish Amazonian
reserves, because the mapping from general institutional quality to specific
spatial designation patterns differs fundamentally across governance regimes.

This finding has two practical implications. For risk assessment, it argues against
deploying a single global risk score without regional validation: such a score would
obscure continent-level variation in the drivers of designation, and whether a global
hierarchical model with tenure and country fixed effects could achieve comparable
performance has not been tested. The findings also suggest
that the most valuable next-generation features are spatially granular institutional
ones: parcel-level land-tenure status (public vs.\ private ownership), which country-level
governance indicators cannot proxy, carbon-market eligibility at the project level,
and NGO funding flows that enable or constrain specific designation processes.

\subsection{Financial Risk Applications}
\label{sec:disc_calibration}
The probabilities produced by this framework are best used as policy-conditioned risk
estimates: they translate historical designation behaviour into a forward risk surface
and make departures from that trajectory visible. Realising this for financial
applications depends on calibration. A raw ranking of pixels by predicted score is
useful for conservation prioritisation, but transition-risk quantification requires
statements of the form ``this parcel has a 3\% probability of being designated within
five years'': an interval-scale probability, not an ordinal rank.

After Platt scaling, the South America RF ECE of 0.0116 (LightGBM: 0.00569) indicates
that calibrated scores approximate observed transition frequencies across probability
bins. The remaining error is not negligible relative to a 0.8\% base rate, so the
maps should be used as calibrated risk inputs, not deterministic parcel forecasts.
A central bank conducting a nature-related stress test could use these probabilities
in a similarly intuitive way. If supervised banks hold agricultural or mining loans
against land parcels with calibrated designation probabilities $\hat{p}$, the central
bank could ask how much collateral value would be at risk if the most exposed parcels
were designated as protected areas within the next five years. In simple terms, a
parcel with a 5\% designation probability and a large expected value discount under
PA restrictions contributes more transition risk than a parcel with a 0.1\% probability.
This thesis provides the spatial probability input for such an exercise, but does not
combine it with real supervisory loan books or collateral valuations.

Knowing where that exposure is concentrated matters as much as
knowing the probability itself. The economic exposure results
(Section~\ref{sec:results_exposure}) show that this varies sharply by region.
In South America and Southeast Asia, the high-risk zone is overwhelmingly composed
of remote forest, shrubland, and savanna: areas that are both ecologically valuable
and cheap for governments to designate, which is precisely why they score highly
under historical designation patterns. The agricultural share of the top-1\% risk
zone is below 2\% in both regions, meaning the transition-risk premium falls almost
entirely on natural-land owners and conservation-adjacent operators rather than
active farming businesses. Much of this high-risk natural land lies outside globally
prioritised conservation areas (Section~\ref{sec:forward_overlap}), reinforcing the
path-of-least-resistance characterisation: governments extend protection into
accessible frontier land, not into contested agricultural or high-biodiversity-value
cores. In the United States, by contrast, the top-1\% zone has an 11.7\% agricultural
share, reflecting the mixed public-agricultural landscape of the West where federal
land and private ranches, grazing permits, and water rights coexist in close
proximity. Designation here can directly restrict working agricultural businesses,
which is why such proposals tend to be more politically contested than the
remote-wilderness expansions typical of South America and Southeast Asia.

The spatial clustering of apparent false positives is itself informative for risk
screening. Backtest analysis at origin $T=2019$ (Appendix, Figure~\ref{fig:fp_backtest})
shows that top-zone pixels not designated within the five-year window concentrate
in known designation frontiers such as the Amazon arc, Andean foothills, and the arid
BLM West, rather than scattering randomly. These pixels are therefore better
characterised as temporally displaced than as genuine misclassifications:
the model correctly identifies active frontier zones, but specific parcels are
designated slightly outside the prediction window. For a risk-screening tool, this
property is desirable: a parcel that scores highly but avoids designation in one
five-year window remains in an elevated-risk zone for the next.

If Kunming-Montreal momentum accelerates designation, BAU will understate realised
risk; if implementation stalls, shifts toward OECMs, or is reversed through legal
challenge, it will overstate it. This is why the scenario maps are presented as
reference trajectories for planning and stress testing rather than as point
predictions of parcel-level legal change. Taken together, the calibrated probability
surface, the regional exposure decomposition, and the scenario maps constitute the
analytical inputs needed for a structured transition-risk assessment; translating
them into portfolio-level expected losses requires external collateral valuations
and loan-book data that are beyond the scope of this thesis.

\subsection{30$\times$30 Feasibility}
\label{sec:disc_forward}
The on-track assessment in Table~\ref{tab:ontrack} delivers a clear message: no region
studied here will reach the 30$\times$30 target on time under business-as-usual
trajectories. South America, despite having the smallest percentage-point gap, would require
approximately 68 additional years from 2024 to reach 30\% at BAU rates (or roughly
62 years past the 2030 deadline); the United States and Southeast Asia face
deficits of more than 170 additional years. All figures refer to formal WDPA PA
coverage; counting OECMs and indigenous territories toward Target~3 would reduce
these shortfalls.

Closing this gap requires not only more designations but different ones. The
scenario maps serve two roles: as a prioritisation tool (high-probability clusters
identify politically feasible near-term targets) and as a gap analysis (comparing
against GSN priority layers or Key Biodiversity Areas reveals which high-priority
areas are \emph{not} predicted to be designated under the modelled trajectory).

The distinction between governments that need to accelerate designation and
those that need to redirect it matters. South America would need to
accelerate back to designation rates of the 2000s, but also redirect to 
higher-priority areas. The same applies to the United States and 
Southeast Asia, which also face substantially larger deficits
and likely require both acceleration and redirection toward ecologically
higher-priority areas.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion_limitations}

\paragraph{Evaluation design:}
The temporal test set is held out from hyperparameter search, model fitting, and
calibration, but the five-year target creates overlapping label windows: one eventual
designation can generate positive labels in several preceding pixel--years. Because
many predictors are static or slow-moving, temporal-test metrics may be optimistic
relative to strict deployment, consistent with known risks in rolling validation
designs \citep{lopezdeprado2018, cerqueira2020}. The $T=2019$ walk-forward backtest
(Appendix~\ref{app:backtest}) is a more conservative deployment analogue.
Spatial dependence creates a related concern: LOBO and cross-regional transfer are
stricter than the temporal test, but cannot fully remove shared continental gradients.
The United States Random Forest result is the clearest special case: its ROC-AUC of
0.999 reflects a small cluster of test positives in BLM landscapes, so the LightGBM
result is the more conservative US benchmark.

\paragraph{Policy stationarity and target definition:}
The model extrapolates historical designation patterns and cannot observe future
structural breaks such as government turnover, carbon-market expansion,
Kunming-Montreal implementation momentum, legal rollbacks, or disclosure-driven
land-use responses \citep{tnfd2023}. This does not make the maps unusable; it defines
their role as reference trajectories for scenario analysis. Target measurement also
matters: WDPA gazette years can lag legal designation \citep{bingham2019}, and OECMs
or indigenous conserved areas may follow different siting logic from conventional PAs
\citep{maxwell2020}. A model trained on formal PA designations may therefore miss
future area-based conservation that is politically feasible precisely because it is not
a classical PA.

\paragraph{Missing and approximate covariates:}
The largest omitted variables are spatially granular institutional predictors:
parcel-level land tenure, carbon-market eligibility, and NGO funding flows. Country
governance indicators cannot proxy these local constraints, especially in mixed
public-private landscapes such as the US West. Several included predictors are also
approximations: WorldClim variables are static 1950--2000 normals, road and energy
infrastructure are treated as fixed snapshots, and the agricultural land-value proxy
uses a 3$\times$ cropland-to-pasture weight derived from US data and applied globally.
These choices likely reduce precision in rapidly changing frontier landscapes and in
regions where climate, infrastructure, or land values have shifted since the source
data were produced.

\paragraph{Sensitivity and interpretation:}
Several modelling choices have not been stress-tested at full continental scale.
Temporal weighting may over-extrapolate a temporary acceleration phase. The
distance-to-PA feature may partly capture multi-year designation processes rather than
independent risk. A formal feature-group ablation has not been conducted, so SHAP
rankings may partly reflect correlated predictor groups rather than independent
contributions. The fixed five-year horizon also means that some apparent false
positives are likely delayed designations in the right frontier but wrong window.
Finally, the framework is predictive rather than causal: SHAP values describe learned
associations, and the appropriate use is spatial risk screening and portfolio
prioritisation, not policy impact evaluation.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This thesis has developed and evaluated a machine-learning framework for predicting
the spatial expansion of protected areas under the 30$\times$30 biodiversity target,
applied to three major world regions. The primary Random Forest model for South
America achieves a ROC-AUC of 0.910 and a PR-AUC of 0.548; it captures 69.1\% of
all unique PA establishment events within the top 1\% of predicted risk
(pixel-year Lift@1\% = 67.6$\times$). These results are directly actionable for
spatial transition-risk screening, and the calibrated probability surface provides
the input needed for financial risk quantification and portfolio aggregation.

Three findings stand out. The driver hierarchy reveals
that economic deterrence (road access, population density, agricultural land value), 
institutional capacity, and ecological priority jointly determine where
PAs are designated; driver profiles are region-specific, with terrain and climate
dominating in Southeast Asia and climate plus energy geography driving the US ranking.
If future designations continue to follow the same accessibility-and-governance logic
as historical ones, closing the 30$\times$30 gap will require active compensation
mechanisms in biodiversity-rich, economically contested areas, not only designating
remote land. The gap between what this model predicts and where designation
\emph{should} go for maximum conservation impact is itself a policy finding; in
South America, only 1.6\% of projected 30$\times$30 designations overlap with GSN
climate-stabilisation priorities. Forward projections confirm that all three studied regions are severely off-track:
South America by approximately 68 additional years from 2024, the United States and
Southeast Asia by more than 170 additional years at current designation rates;
OECMs would reduce these figures.

Cross-continental transfer is better interpreted as a robustness diagnostic than as
the main result. Its mixed pattern reinforces the central finding: protected-area
expansion is predictable within regions, but the learned spatial rule is not a
universal template. Some transfer pairs perform well, especially South America to
Southeast Asia under LightGBM, while others are near-random or anti-correlated. For a
global governance framework like Kunming-Montreal, this means that a shared coverage
target can still produce regionally distinct implementation paths. For transition-risk
scoring, cross-regional models require transfer-specific validation and calibration;
where possible, region-specific models remain the more credible basis for local risk
assessment.

For governments, the most actionable output is the scenario maps: they identify
specific geographic clusters that would need to be designated under BAU, moderate,
and full 30$\times$30 scenarios, and can be compared against biodiversity priority
maps to distinguish between acceleration-only and acceleration-plus-redirection
policy needs. For investors and lenders, the calibrated probability surface provides
a transition-risk score aggregable to parcel, watershed, or portfolio level,
providing a spatial input for nature-related financial disclosure wherever
parcel boundary data exist.

The most consequential missing variable is parcel-level land tenure. Public land can
often follow different legal and political designation pathways from private,
concessionary, Indigenous, or community-governed land. Country-level governance scores
cannot make this distinction, but adding parcel-level land tenure data would.
Carbon-market eligibility registers and NGO funding flows would add two further
institutional channels that may shape where 30$\times$30 implementation is feasible.
Recalibrating annually as new WDPA vintages are released would keep the probability
surface aligned with the current pace of policy rather than the historical average.

The tabular gradient-boosting framework treats every pixel-year as an isolated point.
A convolutional or graph-based spatial encoder would instead learn where
protection spreads as a continuous field, capturing spatial dependence that tabular
models can only approximate through engineered neighbourhood features. A temporal sequence model applied over each pixel's
annual history would distinguish locations on an accelerating trajectory from those
stalling, information that is invisible to any single-year snapshot. These are not
incremental refinements to the current setup; they address the two
structural assumptions the tabular approach imposes by construction: that space is
flat and that time is memoryless.

A single model covering every terrestrial biome on Earth is the natural extension 
of this work. One map, updated annually, showing where the next wave of 
protected-area designation is most likely to arrive. Three continents already
demonstrate that the pipeline is technically viable and that the risk scores
translate to policy and financial screening. A global version would give
policymakers, conservation organisations, and financial institutions a common,
data-driven baseline for the defining land-use question:
\emph{which land will be protected, and which will not?}
